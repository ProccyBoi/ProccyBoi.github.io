Engineering notes

Problem

Make real-time rail activity readable on a 291-pixel PCB, with the copper, solder mask and silkscreen forming the map itself.

Design decisions

The service builds station-and-route keys from static GTFS data and reduces realtime vehicle positions to a compact set of LED states for the Wi-Fi-connected board.

Feed caching, per-feed retry backoff and last-good-data expiry keep the update path bounded when an upstream feed fails or rate-limits requests.

Evidence and validation

The photographs show the assembled board and illuminated routes. The public service repository includes tests for feed caching, backoff and stale-data expiry.

Outcome

A 300 × 305.7 mm physical rail display with public service and firmware source. Its PCB-as-map approach later informed the smaller TramTrace layout.

Sydney rail display

I wanted Sydney's rail network to live on the wall, not in another browser tab. Metroboard is a 300 × 305.7 mm PCB with 291 LEDs, laid out as a physical map of the network.

The board joins Wi-Fi and gets TfNSW GTFS real-time data through a service I wrote. Each update turns vehicle positions into light, so the useful part of the display is visible before I have even picked up my phone.

The PCB is the interface

There is no separate screen or printed overlay. The copper, solder mask, silkscreen and LEDs have to work together as both electronics and map graphics.

That constraint is what later led to TramTrace. The tram network needed a different scale and much tighter route geometry, but the idea stayed the same: make live transport data legible as a piece of hardware.
