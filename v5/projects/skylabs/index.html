01 / The work

From aircraft sensors to a flight record

My contribution

I developed the STM32 aircraft logger, ESP32 LoRa ground station and post-flight replay tools. I joined Skylabs in August 2025 and became Avionics & Payload Team Lead in May 2026.

The task

Sensors update at different rates, the radio carries a smaller live subset, and the team still needs a useful record after landing.

Design decisions

Keep the aircraft binary log separate from the live link. Record the received packets again on the ground, then use one data contract for synchronized replay.

02 / Evidence

What the work
shows.

What has been checked

The published F110211 flight contains 13,772 rows over 679.33 seconds. Its report records a 50.0 ms log interval, 13,402 log overruns and zero SD write errors.

What remains unverified

40 Hz is the aircraft logger’s target. This flight does not demonstrate sustained 40 Hz logging; zero SD write errors does not mean every timing deadline was met.

My work

I joined UNSW Skylabs in August 2025 and became Avionics & Payload Team Lead in May 2026. I represented UNSW at AIAA Design/Build/Fly 2026 in Wichita.

My work covers both ends of the link: the STM32 aircraft logger, the ESP32 LoRa ground station and the replay tools used after landing.

Logging and flight replay

The aircraft binary log is the high-rate record, with a 40 Hz target. A smaller telemetry packet crosses the 915 MHz link at up to 10 Hz, where the ground station records it again and forwards a live stream over USB or its own Wi-Fi dashboard.

After the flight, the same data contract feeds a replay tool with the 3D route, attitude, artificial horizon, altitude, speed and GPS readouts kept on one timeline. Treating the boards, packet format and analysis software as one system has made faults much easier to trace.
