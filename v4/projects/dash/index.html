Engineering record

The thinking behind the build.

From the car to the desk

Dash started as the vehicle-mounted safe-driving prototype shown in the photos below. I kept the useful hardware ideas—the ESP32, IMU, touch input, OLED, speaker and battery supply—but rebuilt the firmware around a different job: helping me stay focused at a desk.

The current version is a cube-shaped study companion. Its screen is a pair of animated eyes, so a tap, shake, change of orientation or completed session gets a reaction without turning the device into another notification-heavy display.

The board underneath

The Driving Companion PCB measures 45.2 × 45.2 mm, with two copper layers on a 1.6 mm board. An ESP32-WROOM-32E connects to an MPU-6050 motion sensor, a four-pin display header and a MAX98357A audio amplifier for the speaker.

USB-C and a CH340C provide the wired programming interface. The battery supply combines TP4056 charging, DW01A / 8205A protection and a TPS62A01 regulator. Three mounting holes define the board's attachment points.

Firmware

A single state machine coordinates the character, focus timer, games and low-power states. The MPU-6050 is sampled at 100 Hz for software tap and orientation detection, while separate tasks look after the OLED, audio, touch input and session timing.

Dash creates its own Wi-Fi access point and serves a captive portal from LittleFS, so a phone can start a study session, see recent stats and change settings without an app or account. Completed sessions are kept in a rolling on-device log, and in-progress sessions can survive a reset through RTC memory.

I also built reaction-time and gesture games, OTA update support and an ESP-NOW layer for experimenting with group sessions between nearby units. Power management drops the ESP32 from 240 to 80 MHz as Dash gets drowsy, then uses deep sleep when it has been left alone.
