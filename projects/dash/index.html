    From the car to the desk

    Dash started as the vehicle-mounted safe-driving prototype shown in the photos below. I kept the useful hardware ideas—the ESP32, IMU, touch input, OLED, speaker and battery supply—but rebuilt the firmware around a different job: helping me stay focused at a desk.

    The current version is a cube-shaped study companion. Its screen is a pair of animated eyes, so a tap, shake, change of orientation or completed session gets a reaction without turning the device into another notification-heavy display.

    Inside the enclosure

    The base and lid form a 50 × 52 × 48 mm enclosure around the black PCB. A four-pin GME12864-80 OLED module faces forward, with the 4 Ω / 3 W box speaker behind the rear grille. Magnets sit at the top, alongside the metal capacitive touch plate.

    A 3.7 V, 1000 mAh LiPo supplies the device through a JST PH connection. The battery representation is approximately 35 × 25 × 10 mm, with an ivory mating plug.

    The 3D view uses the supplied housing meshes and PCB design. The other parts are dimensioned visualizations guided by the build photos; their placement, including the battery, illustrates the assembly rather than verifying manufacturing fit.

    The board underneath

    The Driving Companion PCB measures 45.2 × 45.2 mm, with two copper layers on a 1.6 mm board. An ESP32-WROOM-32E connects to an MPU-6050 motion sensor, a four-pin display header and a MAX98357A audio amplifier for the speaker.

    USB-C and a CH340C provide the wired programming interface. The battery supply combines TP4056 charging, DW01A / 8205A protection and a TPS62A01 regulator. Three mounting holes define the board's attachment points.

    The firmware underneath

    A single state machine coordinates the character, focus timer, games and low-power states. The MPU-6050 is sampled at 100 Hz for software tap and orientation detection, while separate tasks look after the OLED, audio, touch input and session timing.

    Dash creates its own Wi-Fi access point and serves a captive portal from LittleFS, so a phone can start a study session, see recent stats and change settings without an app or account. Completed sessions are kept in a rolling on-device log, capped at 64 KiB. The session module accounts for elapsed time across running and paused states.

    I also built reaction-time and gesture games, OTA update support and an ESP-NOW layer for experimenting with group sessions between nearby units. Power management drops the ESP32 from 240 to 80 MHz as Dash gets drowsy. The current firmware uses light sleep so the motion interrupt on GPIO19 can wake it; that pin cannot provide the same wake path from deep sleep.
